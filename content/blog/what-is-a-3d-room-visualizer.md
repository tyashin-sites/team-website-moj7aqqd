Nobody buys a sofa. They buy a living room, and the sofa is the first decision in it. A product page, however good its 3D viewer, can only sell that first decision. The rug that goes under it, the lamp beside it, the artwork above it and the second chair facing it are sold somewhere else, later, or not at all.

A 3D room visualizer is the page that sells the rest of the decisions. This post explains what one is, how it is different from the "view in your room" button you may already have, and the handful of things that separate a usable one from a toy.

## The one-sentence definition

A 3D room visualizer is a browser-based tool that lets a shopper place a brand's real products into a three-dimensional room, at their true size, change the room's floor, walls, lighting and layout around them, and view the composed result from any angle, including in augmented reality on a phone.

Three words in that sentence carry the weight: *real*, *true* and *composed*.

- **Real** means the products are the brand's own catalogue, in the finish and size the shopper picked, not a stock lookalike from a generic furniture library.
- **True** means a 203 cm sofa is 203 cm in the room. If the planner normalises every model to a convenient size, the plan view it draws is fiction.
- **Composed** means more than one product at once, with the relationships between them (does the rug clear the sofa legs, does the pendant hang over the table) visible.

## How it differs from the AR button on your product page

Most brands with 3D on their product pages already have a "view in your room" button. It is genuinely useful, and it answers exactly one question: *does this one product fit here?*

A room visualizer answers the next question: *does this product go with the other products?* That is a merchandising question, not a fit question, and it is the one that decides whether the basket has one item in it or four.

The two are complementary rather than competing. The product page AR is the shopper's first contact with scale. The room is where the brand gets to sell a look. ## What a shopper actually does in one

Walk through the flow on a hosted Shop the Look page:

1. **Browse** the brand's published catalogue. Search by name or SKU, filter by category.
2. **Customize and add.** The product opens in the configurator. The shopper picks the fabric, the size, the modules. That exact configuration is placed into the room.
3. **Arrange.** Drag, rotate, duplicate. Choose a room style (a bright showroom, a cosy bedroom, a Scandinavian living room) and the placed products stay put while the shell changes around them.
4. **Dress the room.** Floor and wall finishes from a material library, a rug from a rug library, plants, wall art, a pendant light that actually lights the scene.
5. **Change the view.** Orbit around it, switch to a top-down plan with the real footprints, or drop to eye height and walk through it.
6. **View it in AR.** The composed room opens in the phone's native AR viewer, Scene Viewer on Android and Quick Look on iOS. On desktop, a QR code hands it to the phone.

Nothing in that list requires an app, an account or a download. That matters more than it sounds: AR apps built by retailers in 2018 were mostly retired because the install step killed the funnel before it started.

## Why furniture and décor brands specifically

Four categories get the most from a room page, for the same underlying reason: the product only makes sense in context.

- **Furniture.** Scale, fabric and finish are judged together, against the other pieces in the room. A single-product photo cannot show any of those relationships.
- **Rugs and carpets.** A rug photographed flat on white is the weakest product image in commerce. A rug under a sofa, with its pile thickness and fringe rendered, is a different product.
- **Lighting.** A lamp is bought for the light it throws, not the shape of its shade. A room that is actually lit by the pendant the shopper placed sells the effect, not the object.
- **Wall art and décor.** Art is bought for a wall. Hanging it on one, at size, above the sofa the shopper just chose, is the whole pitch.

The brands that participate in exhibitions get a second use: a tablet on the booth running the room page replaces the printed catalogue, and the QR hands the visitor's own room to their own phone before they leave the stand.

## What to look for in one

If you are evaluating a room visualizer for your own storefront, the questions that matter are the ones that separate the merchandising tool from the design toy.

**Is it your catalogue?** Generic planners ship with a library of generic furniture. Pretty, and unsellable. The room must be populated from your published products, and only those.

**Are the products configurable inside the room?** If the shopper has to accept the default fabric to place the sofa, the room is showing them something they are not going to buy. The configurator has to run inside the room page and the configured result has to be what gets placed.

**Is scale real?** Ask for the dimensions of a placed product and check them against the spec sheet. Ask to see the plan view. If the sofa and the side table are the same length in the plan, the planner is normalising.

**Can you curate?** The brand should be able to build and publish looks from its own admin, so that the shopper's first experience is a room the brand styled, not an empty box.

**Does it inherit your brand?** Logo, accent colour, currency, whether prices show. A room page that looks like the vendor's product rather than yours is a demo, not a channel.

**Does AR cover the room, not just one product?** The point of composing a room is to see the composition. If the AR step only exports one item at a time, the shopper is back to the product-page experience with extra steps.

## What it is not

A room visualizer is not an interior design service, a CAD tool or a replacement for the product page. It will not render your products if you do not have 3D models of them, and it cannot make a badly modelled sofa look good. It also will not do your merchandising for you: the curated looks are only as good as the person who styles them.

It is a page where the second, third and fourth items in the basket get sold. That is a narrower claim than "immersive experience", and a more useful one.
