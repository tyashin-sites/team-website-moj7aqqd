These three get sold as a bundle and evaluated as a bundle. That is why so many projects buy all three, deploy all three, and use one.

They solve genuinely different problems. Your catalogue probably has two of those problems, not three, and knowing which two is the difference between a project that pays back and one that becomes a line item somebody questions next budget round.

## The three problems, stated as customer questions

Forget the technology for a moment. Each tool exists to answer one question a customer is silently asking.

**"What does it actually look like?"** — they cannot see the product properly from three photographs. This is a [360° viewer](/360-product-viewer).

**"What would *mine* look like?"** — the product has options, and the photograph shows a combination they are not buying. This is a [configurator](/3d-product-configurator).

**"Will it work in my space?"** — they cannot judge size, or how the finish behaves in their light. This is [AR](/ar-viewer).

If your customers are not asking one of these, the corresponding tool will be admired in the demo and ignored in production.

## A decision table you can actually apply

| If your catalogue... | You need | You probably don't need |
|---|---|---|
| One fixed build, sold on detail and craft | 360° viewer | Configurator |
| Many finishes and sizes, bought online | Configurator + 360° | — |
| Large physical items, any option count | AR, plus whichever of the other two fits | — |
| Made-to-order, quoted not carted | Configurator (with quote output) | Cart integration |
| Small, cheap, unambiguous items | Probably none of them | All three |

That last row is worth saying out loud. A £12 desk accessory does not have a visualisation problem. Nobody hesitates, nobody returns it because it was not what they pictured, and no amount of 3D changes the economics. Deploying this on a catalogue of cheap unambiguous goods is the most reliable way to conclude 3D "doesn't work".

## Where each one earns its keep

### 360° viewer

The right answer when the product is fixed but **misunderstood**. Craft goods, machinery, hardware, instruments — anything where the buying decision rests on detail a photographer did not think to capture.

The tell is your support inbox. If people keep asking "what does the back look like", "how does the hinge work", "is that a join or a shadow" — those are questions a viewer with anchored hotspots answers permanently, and a photograph answers one at a time by email.

Where it disappoints: on a product whose options are the whole conversation. Showing one configuration beautifully does not help someone choosing between forty.

### 3D configurator

The right answer when **options are the friction**. Furniture, kitchens, doors, windows, machinery, custom luggage — anywhere a dropdown is asking the customer to imagine something you have not shown them.

Two effects, and the second is usually the underrated one. On the front end, the buyer sees their build rather than a representative build. On the back end, the configuration leaves as structured data — a specification, a quote, a bill of materials — instead of as a screenshot and a phone call, which removes a whole class of manufacturing rework.

Where it disappoints: when it is installed as a visual toy with no connection to the cart or the quote. A configurator that changes the picture but not the order is a demo you are paying for monthly.

### AR

The right answer when the product is **physically consequential**. Furniture, wardrobes, doors, sanitaryware, large appliances, machinery.

AR answers scale and fit — questions a photograph structurally cannot answer, because a photograph has no relationship to the customer's room. It partly answers finish-in-context. It does not answer comfort, weight or build quality, and implying otherwise just relocates the disappointment to delivery day.

Where it disappoints: on small items, and on any product where the model was not built to true dimensions. AR places objects at real-world scale, so a model that is approximately proportioned is exposed immediately.

## The dependency nobody mentions in the sales call

All three run on the same asset.

One well-built model — correct dimensions, physically based materials, properly compressed — drives the viewer, the configurator and the AR step. There is no separate "AR model" to commission.

This has a practical consequence for sequencing. **The expensive, slow, quality-determining part is the model.** The three experiences on top of it are configuration. So the question "which one should we buy?" is less consequential than it sounds, and the question "is the model good enough?" is more consequential than it sounds.

It also means the sensible order of operations is: build one excellent model, switch on the experience that answers your customers' loudest question, measure, and add the others when the evidence says so. The [asset preparation](/blog/how-to-prepare-a-3d-model-for-the-web) is where the risk lives.

## A shortcut if you only have five minutes

Go and read the last fifty pre-sales questions your team answered.

- Mostly "what does it look like from…" → **360° viewer**
- Mostly "can I get it in…" or "what would it cost with…" → **configurator**
- Mostly "will it fit…" or "how big is…" → **AR**

That list is a better specification than any vendor's feature matrix, because it is your customers describing their own hesitation in their own words. It is also free, and you already have it.

## What to do first

Pick the single product where those questions cluster hardest. Model it properly. Switch on the one experience that answers the dominant question. Put it in the main gallery, not in a tab. Leave it for a full purchase cycle, then look at add-to-cart rate and — later, and more importantly — return rate.

Then buy the second thing with your own data instead of someone else's case study.
